Outreach & Leadership

Building the community the next decade of Rubin will need

Rubin Observatory sits on a mountain in Chile and runs on a partnership that has to hold for thirty years. Most of my outreach work is about strengthening the human and political infrastructure that survey astronomy quietly depends on.

2024 — present Co-founder & program lead KIPAC · Universities across Chile

KIPAC-Chile Engagement Program

Launched through a KIPAC Innovation Grant, the program gives KIPAC members travelling to Chile access to dedicated funds for building professional and personal connections with scientists at Chilean institutions — and for starting research collaborations that would not otherwise happen. I co-founded it, manage its budget, and organized visiting-speaker travel to every university in Chile with a PhD program in astronomy or physics. We also designed new metrics to track engagement and measure impact over the program's first two years.

Why it matters. Rubin's host country is also its most important long-term scientific partner. Chilean astronomers are the constituency that argues for dark-sky protection in national policy, and light pollution — from mining installations, from the rapid growth of La Serena and Coquimbo, from satellite constellations overhead — is the single most controllable threat to the Simonyi Survey Telescope's photometric depth. Sustained, reciprocal collaboration with Chilean institutions is what turns a US facility sited in Chile into a Chilean scientific asset worth defending. That distinction is precisely what will decide whether the site is still dark enough to observe from in 2036.

2026 SOC / LOC organizer Rubin 2036 Workshop

Rubin 2036 Workshop

A workshop dedicated to planning the future survey strategy and instrumentation for the Simonyi Survey Telescope after LSST's ten-year survey concludes. I serve on both the scientific and local organizing committees, shaping a program that ran from the 2036 astrophysics landscape and the Decadal Survey through concrete LSSTCam upgrade options and lessons from contemporaneous surveys.

Why it matters. Decisions about what comes after LSST are being made now, a decade ahead, because focal-plane upgrades and survey strategy have long lead times. Getting the instrumentation case right in 2026 is what makes an ambitious 2036 possible — and the calibration and systematics work I do on LSSTCam today is exactly the evidence base those upgrade decisions rest on.

2025 Lead organizer La Serena, Chile

La Serena Rubin Science Workshop

A workshop on early Rubin science and its synergies with other NSF, NOIRLab and AURA facilities, bringing project team members together with colleagues from Universidad de La Serena and the surrounding institutions. I led the organization end to end.

Why it matters. Holding the meeting in La Serena rather than in California put first-light science in front of the community living closest to the telescope, and gave local students and faculty a direct route into Rubin data and Rubin collaborations.

2019 — present Mentorship & teaching KIPAC · UC Irvine · UC Davis

Mentoring and teaching

I mentor visiting undergraduate researchers at KIPAC on projects in Rubin instrumentation and survey data analysis. Earlier, I was a teaching assistant for mathematical methods for physics at UC Irvine and tutored physics at both UC Irvine and UC Davis.

Why it matters. Instrumentation and calibration are learned by apprenticeship — there is no textbook for why a particular sensor misbehaves. Bringing students into that work early is how the expertise survives a generation of the survey.

2023 — present Active member San Francisco Bay Area

Save The Bay

Permanent active member, working on political advocacy, public education, habitat restoration and conservation of native wildlife in the San Francisco Bay Area.

Why it matters. The same argument runs through all of this: environmental conditions that look like background — a dark sky, a functioning wetland — stay that way only because people organize to keep them.
